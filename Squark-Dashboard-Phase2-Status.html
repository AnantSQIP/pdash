BuiltWhat I made this round

Row 09DepartmentsDone

The API for this has existed since Phase 1 and the web client called exactly one method of it — list. So departments could be read and never created, and the card carried a "Manage in Admin →" link to a page that has no department management on it. Meanwhile the People table showed a Department column computed from job titles by a switch statement, which looked like the feature working and would have disagreed with the real records the moment anybody made one.

What you can now do

Create→Rename / describe→Add members→Name a head→Remove / delete

The panel also lists anybody in no department at all, because a structure covering half the firm looks complete on screen while quietly leaving people out of anything scoped by it.

What I had to add to the API

  • PATCH /:id and DELETE /:id — neither existed, so a department could be created and then never renamed or removed.
  • A case-insensitive duplicate-name guard answering 409 with the clashing name, rather than letting a database constraint surface as a 500.
  • Soft-deleted people excluded from reads. They were not, so somebody offboarded stayed on the card and in its member count indefinitely.
  • Delete refuses while projects are attached; membership cascades away, because a membership means nothing once the department is gone, but a project pointing at one is real attribution.
  • Removing the head clears the headship in the same transaction.

The head was a regular expression

/head|lead|manager/i run over each member's free-text role. "Team Leader" matched on lead. A coordinator titled "Manager, vendor relations" matched on manager. Where two people matched, the winner was whoever joined first. Who runs a department is a decision somebody makes, so it is a column now.

The migration backfills exactly what the regex would have chosen, so nothing is lost, and de-duplicates names before creating the unique index — otherwise it would fail on data already in your table.

Row 08The org chartDone

The Company page has said "Announcements, HR policies and the org chart" in its subtitle since the module was built. There was no org chart. There could not have been — user_manager was empty until Phase 2 wrote the lines, so a chart would have drawn 26 disconnected people.

It draws from flat edges rather than a nested tree, because the chart legitimately has more than one root: the person at the top, plus anyone whose line is not yet recorded. On your data: 27 people, one root, deepest chain five levels — Mohit → Nitin → Ajay → Amritpal → Aman.

Testing it found a bug in my own code

I planted a cycle — two people managing each other — to check the chart survived it. It did not crash. It did something worse: 11 of 27 people silently disappeared. My "not placed" group only caught people whose manager was empty, and someone caught in a loop has a manager, so they and everyone beneath them simply were not drawn, with nothing to say so.

Placement is decided by reachability now, and everyone the tree cannot hold is named in one of two groups — no line recorded or caught in a loop — because the causes and the fixes are different. Re-tested: 27 of 27 accounted for.

Row 05Ten policiesDone

You are right that you never gave me any, and I am not going to write your staff handbook. So these are something narrower and defensible.

Each one describes a rule the software already enforces, with the source cited so anybody can check it. Nothing is invented HR policy. The numbers are read from the implementation:

  • A day under 4 hours is a half day — HALF_DAY_HOURS
  • Timesheets: free to 31 days, Super Admin approval to 92, blocked after — SELF_FILL_DAYS, APPROVAL_MAX_DAYS
  • 12 casual, 8 sick, 15 earned — the configured leave quotas
  • 2 optional holidays a year — your published calendar
  • WFH approved by holders of attendance.manage — HR, Admin, Super Admin

The ten cover attendance and punching, working from home, leave, holidays, comp-off, timesheets, client confidentiality and patent handles, expenses, personal data and who can see it, and how an appraisal runs.

Acknowledgement is deliberately switched off on all of them. Acknowledging a policy records that somebody accepted a term of employment; asking 27 people to sign a description of software behaviour before HR has read it would make that record mean less, not more. HR turns it on per policy once they have reviewed. Every one is editable and deletable like any other.

One is deliberately incomplete. The expenses policy states the workflow the system enforces and then says plainly that the firm's spending limits are not encoded anywhere and need filling in by HR. I would rather ship a policy that admits a gap than one that invents a number.

TestingWhat I ran, and what it found

Everything below was executed against a real API on a scratch database, signed in as six different people. Not a code read.

On "A/B testing": I did not do it, and it is worth saying why rather than quietly substituting something else. A/B testing means showing two variants to real users and comparing behaviour — it needs live traffic and a measurable outcome. What is useful here instead is what I ran: permission testing across roles, boundary and edge cases, and deliberately hostile data. If what you meant was "test it every way you can", that is what follows.

The migration is safe against your live data: it de-duplicates names before creating the unique index, so it cannot fail on rows already there, and it backfills each department's head to whatever the old regex would have picked.

The policy seed is idempotent and matches on title — so if HR later rewrites a policy but keeps its title, re-running would overwrite their version. Run it once, then leave it alone.
